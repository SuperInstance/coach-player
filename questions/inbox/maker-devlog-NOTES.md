# Maker Lane — Devlog Personalities: mining notes

15 questions (maker-001 to maker-015), all hypothesis grade with
`winner_lean: null`. No transcripts were available this round, so
nothing here is shaded; every `evidence_needed` names the maker
channels whose videos would settle it (Juniper Dev, CodyCantEatThis,
Barji, Its Rascal, NathanOnDev, Zoteling, RookieDev). The mining
angle: a maker on camera is constantly *deciding in public* — what
to add, what to cut, what to believe — and those decisions are the
part of game-making a player never sees. Where a question also
changes the avatar's live behaviour in someone else's game, mode
is "both" rather than "maker".

## Top 5

1. **maker-003 — bug fix vs bug canon.** The sharpest split in the
   family. Every maker hits unintended behaviour; some patch it on
   principle, some name it and build the game around it. It
   discriminates because it is a values call, not a skill call, and
   it is immediately usable: the avatar will meet exploits in live
   play and its commentary needs a stance before that moment.
2. **maker-011 — where the fun lives (30-second loop vs long arc).**
   The maker's master reading question, and the one the avatar uses
   most directly: naming a new game's loop or its arc in the first
   minutes determines its commentary, its risks and the whole
   session shape. Narrow spread by design — makers tend to commit
   hard to one answer.
3. **maker-001 — scope discipline (bolt it on vs park it).** The
   mid-build idea is a universal event in devlogs, so evidence is
   everywhere, and the two poles produce visibly different channels:
   the sprawling project that eats its maker vs the frozen plan that
   ships. It also gives the coach curriculum its clearest
   maker-lesson: a feature freeze performed in public.
4. **maker-005 — playtest stance (silent observer vs explainer).**
   The maker's version of the coach problem. Watching a stranger
   fail at your own game and *not* helping is a discipline some
   makers have and some visibly cannot perform, which makes it a
   clean discriminator — and it reflects straight back onto the
   product: when should our avatar let its coach fail?
5. **maker-010 — constraint as enemy vs constraint as co-designer.**
   One roster maker has built an entire channel on the high pole,
   which anchors the spectrum and makes the low pole measurable
   everywhere else. Live, it decides whether the avatar rages at a
   game's limits or plays them as the real rules.

## Considered and dropped as flat

- **"Does the maker comment their code / keep it tidy?"** Real
  makers split on it, but the answer changes nothing the avatar
  says, does or risks on screen. Fails the moves-behaviour test;
  it is production trivia.
- **"Game jam vs long project — which does the maker prefer?"**
  A format preference, not a behaviour spectrum: the same maker
  answers it differently per season, and a Jev judgment would sit
  near 50 because the question measures circumstance, not stance.
- **"Does the maker actually like their own game?"** Unfalsifiable
  sentiment. Devloggers perform both love and frustration for the
  camera, so segments cannot shift an estimate honestly. It is
  philosophy, not a database question.
- **"How many hours a day does the maker work?"** A number, not a
  spectrum of decisions; it describes a life circumstance (day job
  vs full-time) — the medium, not the character.
- **"Solo dev vs team?"** Same failure as hours: circumstance, not
  stance, and it collapses the moment a solo dev hires a composer.
